Tuguegarao City

Pancit Batil Patung

Pancit Batil Patung was originated and developed by the local Ibanag people of Tuguegarao City, Cagayan, tracing its roots back to the 1960s. The most famous culinary landmark of Cagayan (specifically Tuguegarao). It features fresh, thick miki noodles stir-fried with minced carabeef (carabao beef), bean sprouts, and cabbage. It is topped with crunchy carajay (pork cracklings), liver, and a poached or fried egg. It is traditionally eaten with a side of batil (egg-drop) soup and chopped raw onions mixed with calamansi and chili.


It has evolved and greatly varied among pansiterias but the same base recipe of the original pancit back in the 70s consist mainly of homemade miki or noodles topped with carabeef since it's cheaper than cattle beef, mung bean sprouts and topped with poached egg. Customers were also given an egg-drop soup as a side with a soy-based sauce with diced onions. Back then, the egg was optional.

Cooking Procedure

Step 1: Prepare the ingredients. Chop the onion and garlic, dice the carrot and celery for the stock, and julienne the carrot for the toppings. Slice the pork liver thinly, shred the cabbage, and prepare the mung bean sprouts. Set aside the beef or carabao bones, minced meat, chicharon, and fresh miki noodles.
Step 2: Prepare the rich stock. Place the beef or carabao bones in a pot with water. Add the beef bouillon cube, white onion, carrot, and celery. Bring to a boil, then simmer until the broth becomes flavorful. Strain the stock and discard the bones and vegetables. Keep the broth warm.
Step 3: Cook the meat and liver. Heat cooking oil in a large pan. Sauté half of the garlic and chopped onion until fragrant. Add the minced carabao meat or ground beef and cook thoroughly. Add the sliced pork liver and cook until done. Season with soy sauce, fish sauce, and black pepper to taste.
Step 4: Add the vegetables and chicharon. Add the julienned carrot, shredded cabbage, and mung bean sprouts to the pan. Stir-fry until the vegetables are slightly tender. Mix in the crushed pork chicharon or lechon carajay bits, reserving some for the topping if desired.
Step 5: Cook the noodles. In another pan, heat the remaining oil and sauté the remaining garlic and onion. Add the fresh miki noodles and stir-fry until heated through. Pour in a little prepared stock and season with soy sauce and black pepper as needed. Cook until the noodles are tender and well coated.
Step 6: Complete the batil broth. Bring the prepared stock to a gentle simmer. Slowly pour in the beaten egg while stirring to create delicate egg strands. Adjust the seasoning with fish sauce or black pepper if needed.
Step 7: Prepare the patung. Fry or poach one egg per serving, according to your preference. The egg will be placed on top of each serving of noodles.
Step 8: Prepare the dipping sauce. Combine the chopped red onions with Philippine vinegar, soy sauce, calamansi juice, and crushed red chilies. Adjust the mixture to your preferred taste.
Step 9: Assemble and serve. Place the cooked noodles on a serving plate. Add the meat and vegetable mixture, then top with the fried or poached egg and reserved chicharon. Serve the hot batil broth in a separate bowl, with the onion-vinegar dipping sauce on the side.

Cabagan, Isabela

Pancit Cabagan

Pancit Cabagan is a famous noodle dish that originated in Cabagan, Isabela, in Cagayan Valley. It is made with thick egg noodles, pork or chicken, vegetables, and a flavorful sauce or broth, often topped with crispy pork lechon carajay and quail eggs. Introduced through Chinese culinary influences and adapted to local tastes, this delicious dish has become one of Isabela's best-known food specialties, representing the province's rich food culture and culinary traditions.

Just like any other noodle dish in the Philippines, Pancit Cabagan is of Chinese origin. In 1887, according to a research conducted, a group of Chinese traders, originally from Amoy (now Xiamen), China, landed in Aparri (Cagayan). Among them was Sia Liang (also known as Diangga) who married Augustina Deray Laddaran, a Filipina, and established a panciteria in Cabagan during the pre-war period, using ingredients that were locally available at that time.

Cooking Procedure

Step 1: Sauté the ingredients. Heat oil in a pan or wok. Sauté the garlic and onion, then add the pork and chicken. Cook until lightly browned.
Step 2: Add the seasonings and broth. Pour in the soy sauce, oyster sauce, and chicken broth. Bring to a boil and simmer until the meat is tender.
Step 3: Cook the noodles. Add the miki noodles and stir gently so they absorb the flavorful broth. Cook according to the noodle package instructions.
Step 4: Add the vegetables. Add the cabbage and carrots. Cook for a few minutes until the vegetables are tender but still slightly crisp. Season with salt and pepper.
Step 5: Garnish and serve. Transfer to a serving plate. Top with boiled quail eggs, crispy pork, and spring onions. Serve hot with calamansi.

Ilocos

Pinakbet

Pinakbet is a traditional Filipino vegetable dish that originated in the Ilocos Region of Northern Philippines. Its name comes from the Ilocano word pinakebbet, meaning “shrunk” or “wilted,” referring to the way the vegetables are cooked. It commonly contains bitter melon, eggplant, squash, okra, string beans, and tomatoes, seasoned with fermented fish paste (bagoong). Although it is best known as an Ilocano specialty, variations are enjoyed throughout the Philippines, including Cagayan Valley.

Traditionally, pinakbet was cooked using a cooking pot, the banga mostly made in San Nicolas, Ilocos Norte. All the ingredients(thin small eggplants, small round ampalaya, sitaw, patani, okra, pallang or winged beans, siling pansigang, no kalabasa) are placed inside the banga, topped with kinalabasang kamatis and chopped bagnet if available and seasoned with bagoong. Once the banga with the ingredients and cover are placed over the stove, never open it nor stir the ingredients. Once or twice thru the cooking, shake the banga using silong to overturn the ingredients inside. Once cooked, serve the viand in a malokong(bowl) with steaming rice and puting adobo/longganisang Ilocano(they go together well). Pinakbet was traditionally quite salty in taste because the saltiness acts as a preservative because in the hot arid climate of the Ilocos region, food spoils rather easily. Farmers took this viands of pinakbet and puting adobo or longganisa or msybe bagnet along with rice for his lunch in the ricefields.

Cooking Procedure

Step 1: Prepare the vegetables. Wash all the vegetables thoroughly. Slice the bitter melon and eggplant, cut the squash into cubes, and trim the okra and string beans.
Step 2: Sauté the ingredients. Heat oil in a pot. Sauté the garlic, onion, and tomatoes. Add the pork, if using, and cook until lightly browned.
Step 3: Add bagoong and water. Add the bagoong and water. Stir well and bring the mixture to a boil.
Step 4: Cook the vegetables. Add the squash first, followed by the string beans, eggplant, okra, and bitter melon. Cover and simmer for about 10–15 minutes, or until the vegetables are tender. Avoid stirring too much to keep them from breaking apart.
Step 5: Serve. Taste and adjust the seasoning if needed. Serve hot with steamed rice.

Cagayan

Sinanta

Sinanta is a traditional Ibanag noodle soup that originated in Cagayan Valley, particularly associated with Tuguegarao City, Cagayan. It is made with flat egg noodles (laddit), rice vermicelli, chicken, pork, small freshwater clams, and a golden broth flavored with annatto (atsuete). More than just a comforting dish, Sinanta represents the rich culinary heritage of the Ibanag people and is traditionally served during family gatherings and special occasions.

The Ibanag language is one of the very few indigenous Philippine languages that natively uses the "F" sound. Culturally, Sinanta is incomplete without its historical partner: Pinakafu. This is a puffed, deep-fried sweet rice cake coated in native molasses that locals dip directly into the savory, orange, annatto-stained broth of the Sinanta, creating a stark sweet-and-savory profile.

Cooking Procedure

Step 1: Prepare the broth. Boil the chicken and pork in water until tender. Remove the meat, shred or slice it, and set aside the broth.
Step 2: Cook the clams. Boil the cleaned clams until they open. Keep the clam broth for added flavor and discard any clams that remain closed.
Step 3: Sauté the ingredients. Heat oil in a pot. Sauté the garlic and onion, then add the pork, chicken, dried shrimp, and annatto powder.
Step 4: Add the broth and noodles. Pour in the meat broth and clam broth. Add the clams and noodles, then simmer until the noodles are tender. Season with fish sauce and pepper.
Step 5: Serve. Transfer to bowls and garnish with chives or spring onions and fried garlic. Serve hot.

Ilocos

Dinengdeng

Dinengdeng, also known as Inabraw, is a traditional vegetable dish that originated in the Ilocos Region of Northern Philippines. It is made with a variety of local vegetables, such as malunggay leaves, okra, eggplant, squash flowers, and string beans, cooked in a broth flavored with fermented fish paste (bagoong). Some versions include grilled or fried fish for extra flavor. This simple and nutritious dish reflects Ilocano culture, resourcefulness, and the tradition of using fresh vegetables grown in home gardens.

Unlike many Filipino dishes influenced by Spain or China, Dinengdeng (historically called Abraw or Inabraw) is a purely indigenous, pre-colonial creation. It traces back to a time when Ilocanos survived primarily by foraging wild flora. The foundational flavor of Dinengdeng relies on saluyot (jute mallow). Foraging ancestors discovered that boiling saluyot leaves creates a mucilaginous (slimy/velvety) broth that acts as a natural throat-soother and coolant during dry, scorching plantation months.

Cooking Procedure

Step 1: Prepare the vegetables. Wash all the vegetables thoroughly. Slice the eggplant, cut the string beans, and prepare the leaves and squash flowers.
Step 2: Prepare the broth. Bring the water to a boil in a pot. Add the tomato, onion, and bagoong. Stir and allow the broth to simmer.
Step 3: Add the vegetables. Add the vegetables that take longer to cook, such as string beans and eggplant, followed by the okra and squash flowers.
Step 4: Add the leafy vegetables. Add the malunggay leaves near the end and simmer until all the vegetables are tender. Avoid overcooking them.
Step 5: Serve. Add grilled or fried fish if desired. Serve hot with steamed rice.

Ilocos

Dinakdakan

Dinakdakan is a traditional Filipino dish that originated in the Ilocos Region of Northern Philippines. It is made from grilled or boiled pork parts, such as the pig's face, ears, and sometimes liver. The meat is chopped and mixed with onions, chili peppers, calamansi juice, and traditionally mashed pig brain, although mayonnaise is commonly used in modern versions. Known for its smoky, savory, and tangy flavor, Dinakdakan is a popular Ilocano delicacy that showcases the region's distinctive cooking traditions.

Long before it became a popular bar food (pulutan), Dinakdakan was known in deep Ilocano mountains as Warek-Warek. It was originally prepared as a ritualistic celebratory dish after a successful hunting expedition or community milestone.

Cooking Procedure

Step 1: Prepare the pork. Clean the pork face and ears thoroughly. Place them in a pot of water and boil until tender.
Step 2: Grill the meat. Remove the pork from the pot and let it cool slightly. Grill over charcoal or a grill pan until lightly charred and smoky. Cook the liver separately if using.
Step 3: Chop the pork. Cut the grilled pork into small bite-sized pieces. Place them in a clean mixing bowl.
Step 4: Mix the ingredients. Add the chopped onions, chili peppers, calamansi juice, salt, and pepper. Add mayonnaise if you prefer a creamy modern version. Mix thoroughly.
Step 5: Serve. Transfer to a serving plate and garnish with additional onions and chili peppers. Serve with warm steamed rice.

Ilocos

Igado

Igado is a traditional Filipino dish that originated in the Ilocos Region of Northern Philippines. It is made with pork tenderloin, pork liver, garlic, onions, soy sauce, vinegar, and vegetables such as bell peppers, carrots, and green peas. Its savory and slightly tangy flavor makes it a well-known Ilocano specialty. The dish reflects the region's culinary tradition of combining pork, organ meats, and simple seasonings to create a flavorful meal.

Before the Spanish arrived, the indigenous name for this dish was Dinaldalem, derived from the Ilocano word dalem, meaning liver. The uniform, matchstick-thin julienne cuts of the meat were designed so that the metallic taste of the iron-rich liver would bleed perfectly into the vinegar, neutralizing the gamey flavor. During the Spanish colonial era, Spanish friars and officers noticed Ilocanos cooking a rich, vinegar-braised dish using strips of pork liver. The Spaniards repeatedly referred to it by its Spanish name, Hígado (which literally translates to "liver"). Local Ilocanos, mimicking the foreign accent, dropped the soft "H" sound, transforming the word into Igado.

Cooking Procedure

Step 1: Prepare the ingredients. Slice the pork and liver into thin strips. Chop the garlic, slice the onion and bell peppers, and cut the carrot into strips.
Step 2: Sauté the pork. Heat oil in a pan. Sauté the garlic and onion until fragrant, then add the pork strips and cook until lightly browned.
Step 3: Add the seasonings. Pour in the soy sauce and vinegar. Let the vinegar simmer briefly before stirring. Add the water, bay leaves, and black pepper. Cover and simmer until the pork is tender.
Step 4: Add the liver and vegetables. Add the pork liver and cook thoroughly. Add the carrots, green peas, and bell peppers. Simmer until the vegetables are tender but not mushy.
Step 5: Serve. Taste and adjust the seasoning. Transfer to a serving dish and serve hot with steamed rice.

Ilocos

Papaitan

Papaitan is a traditional Filipino soup known for its distinct bitter taste. It is commonly made with beef or goat innards, such as tripe and intestines, mixed with ginger, garlic, onion, and bile, which gives the soup its signature bitterness. It is strongly associated with Ilocano cuisine in Northern Luzon and is also enjoyed in other parts of the Philippines.

Papaitan's origin is tied directly to the traditional Ilocano kaarruba (neighborhood) system of celebration. When a family slaughtered a goat or cow for a wedding or fiesta, the prime cuts of meat were gifted to guests or reserved for the main feast. The hosts, left only with the bitter bile sac (apdo) and tangled loops of inner organs, refused to throw them away.

Cooking Procedure

Step 1: Prepare the ingredients. Clean the beef or goat innards thoroughly and cut them into small pieces. Chop the garlic and onion, then slice the ginger and green chili.
Step 2: Boil the meat and innards. Place the meat and innards in a pot with water. Bring to a boil and remove any foam from the surface. Simmer until the meat becomes tender.
Step 3: Sauté the aromatics. Heat oil in a pan. Sauté the garlic, onion, and ginger until fragrant. Add the boiled meat and innards, then stir in the fish sauce.
Step 4: Add the broth and seasonings. Pour in some of the meat broth and let it simmer. Gradually add properly sourced, food-safe bile to achieve the desired bitter taste. Season with salt, pepper, and green chili if desired.
Step 5: Serve. Allow the soup to simmer for a few more minutes until the flavors combine. Transfer to a serving bowl and serve hot with steamed rice.

Ilocos

Laoya

Laoya is a traditional boiled-meat soup associated with the food culture of Cagayan and parts of Northern Luzon. It is a comforting dish prepared by simmering pork or beef with ginger, onion, and other seasonings until the meat becomes tender. Depending on the household, vegetables and other ingredients may also be added. Its simple broth highlights the natural flavor of the meat.

The word Laoya is a direct phonetic evolution of the Spanish phrase La Olla, meaning "the ceramic stewing pot." During the Spanish occupation, the ruling elite claimed the prime, tender muscles of beef and pork for their elegant dishes. The local Ilocano working class was left with the bony, undesirable castoffs specifically the tough pork trotters (pata), knuckle bones, and tough tails.

Cooking Procedure

Step 1: Prepare the ingredients. Wash the pork or beef and cut it into serving-sized pieces. Slice the ginger, chop the garlic, and quarter the onion. Prepare potatoes or other vegetables if desired.
Step 2: Boil the meat. Place the meat in a pot with water and bring to a boil. Remove any foam from the surface to keep the broth clear.
Step 3: Add the aromatics. Add the ginger, garlic, and onion to the pot. Cover and simmer over low heat until the meat becomes tender.
Step 4: Season the broth. Add fish sauce, salt, and ground black pepper. If desired, add potatoes or other vegetables and cook until tender.
Step 5: Serve. Taste the broth and adjust the seasonings as needed. Transfer the soup to a serving bowl and serve hot with steamed rice.

Ilocos

Balatong

Balatong, also known as monggo, is a traditional Filipino mung bean dish commonly cooked with garlic, onion, tomatoes, and leafy vegetables. Some versions include pork, fish, or dried fish for extra flavor. In Ilocano and other Northern Luzon communities, balatong is a familiar, affordable home-cooked meal often served with rice.

The story of cooking Balatong (mung beans) specifically on Fridays is deeply rooted in the religious history of Spanish Catholicism. The Catholic Church enforced a strict rule of abstinence from eating meat on Fridays as a form of penance. Because meat was forbidden, Ilocano mothers turned to mung beans, which they discovered could be boiled down until thick, creating a dense, savory porridge that mimicked the heavy satiety of a meat dish. To keep it fully compliant with fasting laws, they strictly avoided pork fat, seasoning the legume mash exclusively with fish bagoong and fresh backyard moringa leaves (marunggay) to provide iron and protein to the farming families.

Cooking Procedure

Step 1: Prepare the ingredients. Wash the mung beans thoroughly. Chop the garlic, onion, and tomatoes. Wash the malunggay leaves or spinach, and slice the pork into small pieces if using.
Step 2: Boil the mung beans. Place the mung beans in a pot with water. Bring to a boil and simmer for about 30–45 minutes, or until the beans become soft. Add more water if needed.
Step 3: Sauté the ingredients. Heat oil in a pan. Sauté the garlic, onion, and tomatoes until soft and fragrant. Add the pork, if desired, and cook thoroughly.
Step 4: Combine and season. Add the sautéed mixture to the pot of cooked mung beans. Stir well, then add fish sauce, salt, and ground black pepper. Simmer for another 5–10 minutes.
Step 5: Add the vegetables and serve. Add the malunggay leaves or spinach and cook until wilted. Transfer the balatong to a serving bowl and serve hot with steamed rice.
